Most agents assume that if they booked the shoot and paid the invoice, the listing photos belong to them. In Canada, that assumption has been out of date for more than a decade. Under the *Copyright Act*, the person who takes a photo is usually the first owner of it, and paying for the work does not change that on its own.

That rarely causes trouble while a listing is live. It tends to surface later: when a photo shows up in a different agent's listing, in a brokerage ad a year on, or on a website the photographer never agreed to. Here is how ownership works and what to sort out before anyone picks up a camera.

## The basic rule: the author owns it first

Section 13(1) of the *Copyright Act* says that, subject to the rest of the Act, the author of a work is the first owner of its copyright. For a photograph, the author is the person who made it. So unless something else applies, the photographer owns the images from the moment they are taken.

There is an exception for employees. Under section 13(3), when a work is made by someone employed under a contract of service, in the course of that employment, the employer is the first owner unless they have agreed otherwise. A photographer on a brokerage's payroll is in a different position from an independent photographer hired for a single listing.

## What changed in 2012

For a long time, Canadian law had a special rule for commissioned photos. Section 13(2) said that when someone ordered a photograph or portrait and paid for it, the person who ordered it was the first owner, absent an agreement to the contrary. That is the rule many people in the industry still have in their heads.

Parliament removed it. The *Copyright Modernization Act*, which received Royal Assent on June 29, 2012, repealed section 13(2) outright. The Act's own summary describes the goal as giving photographers the same rights as other creators. The consolidated *Copyright Act* still shows section 13(2) as repealed, and the federal consolidation lists no amendments to the Act since November 2024.

A 2017 CREA Café article made the practical point: if a brokerage hires a photographer to take listing photos, the photographer likely owns the copyright in them. The article also suggests reviewing older photographer agreements from before the change.

## Ownership versus permission

Not owning a photo does not mean you cannot use it. The law separates owning copyright from having permission to use the work. The owner can transfer the copyright outright, which is an assignment, or keep it and grant a licence that lets someone else use the images in agreed ways.

The key detail is in section 13(4): an assignment or licence is not valid unless it is in writing and signed by the owner of the right, or the owner's authorized agent. A handshake, a text saying "use them however you like" or a paid invoice with no terms is a weak foundation if a dispute ever comes up.

CREA's guidance is to have a contract that either assigns copyright to the brokerage or licenses the brokerage to use, display and reproduce the photos. CREA has published a template, and notes that any template should be reviewed by a lawyer before you rely on it.

## The reuse trap

A common scenario: a home comes back to market with a new agent, and the old photos are still sitting on a drive somewhere. Or a colleague offers you the photos from their listing of the same property.

A 2021 CREA Café article by CREA's general counsel walks through exactly this. The agent who hands over the photos may believe they can give permission when they never had the rights to give. The photographer can then come looking for payment, and taking the photos down afterwards does not necessarily end the claim for use that already happened. The article notes that some MLS systems may allow members to reuse listing photos under their own rules, so check with your board, and recommends getting written assurances before reusing any photos, whatever the source.

## What to settle before the shoot

A short written agreement covers most of this. Before booking, make sure you know:

- **Who owns the copyright** after delivery: the photographer, the brokerage or the agent.
- **What the licence covers**: MLS and listing sites, the brokerage website, print, social media and paid ads.
- **How long it lasts**: the life of the listing, a set period, or indefinitely.
- **Whether it transfers**: if the seller relists with another brokerage, can the new agent use the photos, and on what terms.
- **Who else can use the images**: the seller, a stager or the builder may want copies too.
- **Video, drone footage and floor plans**: confirm they are covered by the same terms as the stills.

Sellers have a stake here as well. If you want to use the photos of your own home later, ask your agent what the agreement allows rather than assuming.
