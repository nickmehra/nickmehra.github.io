When you hire a photographer or videographer for a launch or a grand opening, the goal is usually marketing: a recap reel, a website gallery, a few social posts. That means guests' faces are being collected and used for a business purpose, and Canada's private-sector privacy law has something to say about it.

Here is what the federal law and the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (OPC) say, and how that translates into planning.

## Does PIPEDA apply to your event?

The *Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act* (PIPEDA) applies to organizations that collect, use or disclose personal information in the course of commercial activities. The OPC says it generally covers private-sector businesses in Ontario, because Ontario is not one of the provinces (Alberta, British Columbia and Quebec) with its own private-sector law deemed substantially similar. Ontario does have a law declared substantially similar, but it applies to health information.

Some situations fall outside it:

- **Non-profits and charities.** The OPC says PIPEDA generally does not apply to them unless they carry out commercial activities that are not central to their mandate and involve personal information. Its commercial activity bulletin adds that non-profit status alone does not decide the question, and that an organization offering something for free can still be engaged in commercial activity. The test is the activity, not the label.
- **Municipalities, schools and hospitals.** The OPC says these are generally covered by provincial laws rather than PIPEDA, though PIPEDA can apply in some cases.
- **Personal use, and journalistic, artistic or literary purposes.** The Act excludes these, but only where there is no other purpose. A promotional gallery for a business has another purpose.

## A photo of a guest is personal information

PIPEDA defines personal information as information about an identifiable individual. The OPC's bulletin on the definition notes that video surveillance capturing a person's image can be personal information, and that the definition does not require the information to be recorded.

Being in a public or semi-public space does not change that. In a 2015 case about airport video footage, the OPC found that footage clearly showing a traveller was her personal information, and that the Act does not exclude images captured in public settings.

## What meaningful consent looks like

Under PIPEDA, organizations should explain why they are collecting information at or before the time they collect it, orally or in writing. Consent is only valid if it is reasonable to expect that the people you are dealing with would understand what they are agreeing to.

The OPC's *Guidelines for obtaining meaningful consent*, issued in 2018 with the Alberta and B.C. commissioners, ask organizations to make four things prominent: what is being collected, who it will be shared with, what it will be used for, and any meaningful risk of harm. For an event: you will be photographed and filmed, the images may appear on our website and social channels, and our photographer will have them.

A few more points from the Act and the guidelines matter for events:

- **Form of consent can vary.** The Act lists options including a signed form, an opt-out checkbox, verbal consent and consent implied by using a service. Implied consent may be enough for less sensitive information.
- **Express consent is the default in harder cases.** The guidelines say express consent is generally required when information is sensitive, when a use is outside what people would reasonably expect, or when there is a meaningful risk of significant harm.
- **Don't make it a condition of entry.** The Act says an organization cannot require consent beyond what is needed for the legitimate purpose as a condition of a product or service. The guidelines call for clear yes-or-no choices.
- **People can change their minds.** Consent can be withdrawn, subject to legal or contractual limits and reasonable notice, and the organization should explain what withdrawing means.

## Children need extra care

The OPC's position is that, except in exceptional circumstances, a parent or guardian must consent for anyone under 13.

A 2025 OPC finding shows how this plays out. A privately run swimming pool required parents to accept a photo waiver, with no opt-out, allowing promotional use of images of their children. The OPC found that images of children in swimwear were sensitive, that the pool should generally have sought express consent, and that making promotional photos a condition of lessons was not allowed. The pool agreed to move to an opt-in process at registration.

When kids are likely to be in the frame, ask parents at registration and make it a real choice, separate from the ticket.

## A planning checklist for organizers

- Decide where the photos and video will be used before the event, and say so in plain words on the registration page or invitation.
- Post clear signage at the entrance and photo areas saying the event is being photographed and filmed, and how to opt out.
- Offer an easy opt-out on the day, such as a coloured lanyard or wristband, and brief your photographer on it.
- Get written opt-in consent from parents for any child who might appear in promotional content.
- Get specific consent before featuring an individual prominently, such as a testimonial clip or a poster image.
- Have a simple way to handle removal requests after the event, and know everywhere the media has been posted.

> Notice tells guests what is happening. Consent gives them a real say in it.
