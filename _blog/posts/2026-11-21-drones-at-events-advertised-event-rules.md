A wide shot from above tells the story of an event in a way no ground camera can: the size of the crowd, the layout of the vendor rows, the start line stretching down the road. It is also the one piece of event coverage that needs federal permission before anyone shows up with gear.

Our [listing drone post](/journal/drone-listing-rules-ontario/) covered weight, registration and pilot certificates. This one is for organizers of public events, where the rules are stricter.

## What counts as an advertised event

The Canadian Aviation Regulations define an advertised event as an outdoor event advertised to the general public, and give concerts, festivals, markets and sporting events as examples. Transport Canada asks pilots to weigh a few factors: whether the event is publicly advertised, how it is promoted, whether the public can get onto the site, and whether the drone will fly within 30 metres of it.

Invitation-only gatherings closed to the public, such as a private birthday party, are not advertised events, and neither is something posted only inside a private online group. A farmers' market, a charity run, a car show or a community festival with posters and social media promotion almost certainly is.

## The permission: an SFOC

Any drone flown at an advertised event needs a Special Flight Operations Certificate for remotely piloted aircraft systems (SFOC-RPAS). That has applied to drones of 250 grams or more since 2019 and to sub-250-gram microdrones since April 1, 2025. Filming or photographing the event falls under this certificate, and so does flying for security or surveillance. A drone show, where the drones are the performance, is handled separately as a special aviation event.

The boundary matters. Flying within 30 metres of the event's edges needs the SFOC. Where there is no clear perimeter, such as a marathon, a boat event or fireworks, Transport Canada expects the boundary to sit at least 30 metres from participants or from a race course. A pilot who stays more than 30 metres outside those boundaries is generally not considered part of the event.

## What the application involves, and how long it takes

For a drone of 250 grams or more, the pilot applying needs an Advanced Operations certificate and a registered drone. The application also needs the drone's make and model and written permission from both the event organizer and the landowner. The operator cannot apply without your letter.

A Transport Canada inspector reviews the file. Requests that meet low-complexity criteria are invoiced, and the certificate is issued once the fee is paid. Fees have applied since November 4, 2025, on a scale set by complexity, starting at $20 for the simplest category. The service standard is up to 30 working days, files are handled in the order received, and an incomplete application or a slow reply to follow-up questions adds time.

Thirty working days is about six calendar weeks, so the drone conversation belongs at the same planning meeting as the venue and the vendor list.

## Near and over the crowd

Your attendees are not part of the flight crew, so the rules treat them as uninvolved people. For drones of 250 grams or more, Transport Canada's advertised-event guidance says flying between 5 and 30 metres from uninvolved people, or within 5 metres of them, requires a drone model covered by a safety assurance declaration for that kind of operation. Not every professional drone has one, so ask.

For a drone of 250 grams or more at an advertised event without the required certificate, Transport Canada lists fines of up to $3,000 for an individual and up to $15,000 for a corporation.

## Check the park, too

Federal permission covers the airspace; the ground can carry its own rules. Many Durham events happen in municipal parks, and local by-laws often restrict flying there:

- **Whitby:** the Public Parks and Park Buildings By-law lists flying an unmanned aerial vehicle in a park as a restricted activity, allowed only with a permit or prior authorization.
- **Oshawa:** the Parks and Facilities By-law makes operating remote-controlled powered devices, model aircraft included, an offence outside a designated area unless a permit authorizes it.
- **Toronto:** the parks chapter of the Municipal Code prohibits powered model aircraft in parks unless authorized by permit. It does not name drones specifically.

If your event permit comes from the municipality, ask whether aerial filming needs to be listed on it.

## Questions to ask your drone operator

- What do you need from me for the SFOC application?
- What certificate do you hold, and is the drone registered?
- Does the drone carry a safety assurance declaration for flying near or over people?
- Where will you take off and land, and how will you keep that area clear?
- What happens if the certificate does not arrive in time, or wind and weather ground the flight?

## Build in a ground-level backup

Even with every permission in hand, wind, rain or a crowded launch area can keep a drone down. Plan so the event is fully documented from the ground: a walking camera at the main moments, a high vantage point such as a balcony or rooftop where the venue allows it, and a shot list that does not depend on aerials.
