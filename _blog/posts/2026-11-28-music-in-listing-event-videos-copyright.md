Music does a lot of work in a short video. A slow piano track makes a walkthrough feel calm; a driving beat makes an event recap feel busy and full. It is also the part of a listing or event video most likely to be used without anyone checking whether it can be.

Earlier we looked at who owns the photos from a shoot. The soundtrack raises a similar question, with a twist: a single recorded song usually carries several separate rights, often owned by different people.

## One song, several copyrights

Canada's Copyright Act treats the pieces of a recorded song separately.

- **The musical work.** The composition itself, music and lyrics. Under section 3, the owner has the sole right to reproduce it and to communicate it to the public by telecommunication, and the Act specifically includes the right to adapt and present it as a cinematographic work. Copyright in a musical work generally lasts for the author's life plus 70 years after the end of the year they die.
- **The sound recording.** The particular recording you hear. Section 18 gives its maker the sole right to publish it for the first time and to reproduce it, along with making it available online. That right usually runs for decades from the year the recording was first made or published.
- **The performer's performance.** Section 15 gives performers their own copyright in their performance, including rights over recordings of it.

Section 27 sets out the basic rule: doing something only the copyright owner may do, without their consent, is infringement. Putting a commercial track under your listing video and posting it online touches more than one of these rights at once.

## What a sync licence is, and where SOCAN fits

Two terms come up whenever music meets video. A law firm's overview of Canadian music licensing from DLA Piper describes a **synchronization licence** as what lets you pair music with a visual medium, and a **master use licence** as what lets you use a specific master recording in a film or video. In practice, using a commercial recording in a video means clearing both the song and the recording.

SOCAN, which represents songwriters and music publishers, says it administers performing rights and reproduction rights for the composition, and that most music uses need both. It also offers a synchronization licence for works in its reproduction rights repertoire, through a form on its licensing page. SOCAN licenses the composition side; the recording side belongs to whoever owns the master. The DLA overview notes that even a cover version, where you re-record the song yourself, still needs a sync licence for the musical work.

A few other points worth knowing:

- **Buying a song is not a licence to use it.** SOCAN points out that buying or legally downloading music gives you the right to play it in private, not in public.
- **Every use is different.** The DLA overview stresses that use cases vary and that collective tariffs can change, so the right path depends on where and how the video will be shown.
- **Infringement can be costly.** The same overview notes that statutory damages can range from $500 to $20,000 per infringement, at the rights holder's choice.

## Practical options for listing and event media

For most agents, sellers, dealerships and organizers, the simplest route is to avoid commercial hits altogether and use music that comes with clear terms for your use.

- **Use licensed library music.** Music libraries sell or license tracks for video use. Read the licence: check whether it covers commercial use, which platforms, and for how long.
- **Commission or use original music.** A track written for you, with the rights agreed in writing, removes the guesswork.
- **Read the platform's terms before relying on its music.** If you plan to add a song inside a social app, check what that platform's own terms allow for business accounts and whether the same clip can be reused on your website or MLS links.
- **Keep the paperwork.** Save the licence or receipt for each track with the video file. If a clip is reused a year later, you will know what it was cleared for.
- **Remember the event itself.** If an event's own sound system was playing a commercial song, that song may be audible in your footage. A highlight reel can be cut with licensed music over it instead.

## Choosing the sound with care

When we edit, the track is chosen to match the pace of the space or the event, and its licence terms are checked before it goes into the timeline, not after. I have come to think of sound as the quiet half of a video. It shapes how a room feels long before anyone notices the music itself, and getting it right, honestly sourced, is part of telling the truth about a place.
