The best part of event coverage often lands a few days later, when the gallery or highlight video goes out to everyone who was there. For agents, it is the open-house follow-up with a link to the listing video. Both are natural, and both can fall under Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) if the message promotes something.

This is not a reason to stop sending them. It is a reason to plan the list and the message before the event, not after.

## When a photo email becomes a "commercial" message

The Government of Canada describes a commercial electronic message (CEM) as a message sent to an electronic address, such as email or text, that has commercial content: marketing, sales, offers or solicitations.

A plain "here are the photos from Saturday" note may not be promotional at all. But many event and listing emails mix purposes. A gallery email that also invites people to the next paid event, a highlight reel with a sponsor's offer, or a follow-up that pitches a listing or an agent's services leans commercial. Where a message is commercial, CASL's three requirements apply.

## The three requirements

According to the CRTC and the Government of Canada's guidance, a CEM needs:

- **Consent.** Express or implied.
- **Identification.** The name of the sender and of anyone on whose behalf it is sent, a mailing address, and a phone number, email or web address. That contact information has to stay valid for at least 60 days after the message is sent.
- **An unsubscribe mechanism.** Clear and easy to use. Unsubscribe requests have to be honoured within 10 business days, at no cost to the recipient.

## Express and implied consent

Express consent is when someone clearly agrees, for example by ticking an opt-in box on a registration form. The guidance says it does not expire unless the person withdraws it, and it can be limited to a type of message, such as a newsletter.

Implied consent exists only in specific situations and is usually time-limited. The CRTC explains that an existing business relationship can come from a purchase within the past two years, or from an inquiry or application within the past six months. For charities, clubs and similar organizations, the guidance describes non-business relationships, including attending a meeting the organization ran within the past two years.

The CRTC also gives a trade-show example: someone who hands you a business card may imply consent, and a follow-up email confirming the conversation may itself be a CEM that needs identification and an unsubscribe link.

One detail catches people out. The CRTC says an email asking for consent is itself a CEM. You cannot use a cold email to ask permission to send more emails.

## Who has to prove it

The guidance is clear that the sender carries the burden of proving consent, whether express or implied. It recommends keeping records of when and how consent was given, and of unsubscribe requests.

## Planning event and listing media around CASL

- **Ask at registration.** Add a clear, unticked opt-in on the sign-up form for "photos and updates from this event" and, separately, for future promotions.
- **Keep the gallery email simple.** If the goal is sharing photos, keep it about the photos. Put promotions in messages sent only to people who opted in.
- **Include identification and an unsubscribe link anyway.** It costs nothing and covers you if the message turns out to be commercial.
- **For open houses,** a sign-in sheet or form with an opt-in line gives you a record. Without one, don't assume a visit alone gives you consent to send promotions.
- **Track consent with the list.** Note where each address came from and when.
