Most conversations about listing and event media are about how the images look. A quieter question is whether everyone can use them. A buyer who relies on a screen reader cannot see a gallery of kitchen photos. A guest who is Deaf or hard of hearing misses everything said in an event recap video without captions.

In Ontario, this is not only a courtesy. For many organizations, it is part of the *Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act, 2005* (AODA). Here is what the web rules say and how they touch the photos and video you publish.

## Who the website rules apply to

The web requirements sit in section 14 of the Integrated Accessibility Standards, Ontario Regulation 191/11. They apply to designated public sector organizations and to what the regulation calls large organizations: businesses and non-profits with 50 or more employees in Ontario.

The Government of Ontario's guidance puts it plainly: since January 1, 2021, public websites of those organizations must be accessible. The obligation falls on whoever controls the site, either directly or through a contract. It covers web content published after January 1, 2012, and the province defines content broadly, including text, images and sounds.

Organizations with fewer than 50 employees in Ontario are not named in section 14. They still have other AODA duties, though. Under section 12, organizations must provide accessible formats and communication supports when someone asks, in a timely way and at no more than the regular cost. The regulation lists captioning and plain language among those supports.

For a large brokerage, a dealership group or an event organization of that size, the listing pages, inventory pages and event galleries on its own website are web content. If a smaller team works with a partner that is over the threshold, it is worth asking what the partner expects from the media you supply.

## The standard: WCAG 2.0, Level AA

Section 14 points to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.0, a W3C standard from December 2008. For large organizations, the schedule ran in two steps: new websites had to meet Level A by January 1, 2014, and all public websites and content had to meet Level AA by January 1, 2021.

Two Level AA items are carved out: live captions and audio descriptions for prerecorded video. The rest of Level AA applies. The regulation also allows an exception where meeting the requirement is not practicable, and it lets organizations weigh things such as whether commercial tools exist.

## Photos: text alternatives

WCAG's first Level A item is text alternatives for non-text content, usually called alt text. Every meaningful image needs a short written description that a screen reader can read aloud.

For a listing, good alt text describes what the photo shows, not what the marketing wants to say. "Kitchen with white cabinets, quartz counters and a window over the sink" is useful. "Stunning chef's dream kitchen" tells a screen reader user nothing about the room. The same discipline helps accuracy: if you would not write it in the description, it should not be implied by the photo.

Level AA also flags images of text. A price, an open house date or an event schedule baked into a graphic should also appear as real text on the page.

## Video: captions and descriptions

For prerecorded video with sound, WCAG Level A requires captions. W3C's guidance explains that captions are more than a transcript of dialogue. They identify who is speaking and include meaningful non-speech sound, such as applause or a sound effect that matters to the story. Captions that drop dialogue or important sounds are listed as a failure.

For prerecorded video with no audio at all, such as a silent walkthrough, Level A asks for either a text description of what the video shows or an audio track that presents the same information.

Level A also asks for an audio description or a media alternative for prerecorded video. One way to meet it is a full text alternative on the page that describes what a viewer would see and hear. A listing tour can be paired with a room-by-room description written for that purpose.

## A simple checklist for your next shoot

- **Ask for alt text with delivery.** Your photographer knows what each frame shows. Short, factual descriptions per image save time at upload.
- **Caption every video with speech or meaningful sound.** If you start from automatic captions, review them, especially names and street names.
- **Write a text description** for tours, drone clips and event recaps, so the content is not locked inside the video.
- **Keep key facts as text.** Prices, dates, addresses and schedules belong on the page, not only in a graphic.
- **Check where the media lives.** Your own site is in scope; ask your web provider how uploads meet WCAG 2.0 AA.

Accessible media is also clearer media. Descriptions force precision, and captions help anyone watching with the sound off. If you have a listing, inventory shoot or event coming up, [book a shoot](/#booking).
