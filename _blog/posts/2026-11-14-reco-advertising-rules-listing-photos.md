Most agents think of listing photos as marketing. The Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO) thinks of them as advertising, and that puts them under the same rules as a sign, a flyer or a paid post.

That matters for anyone who shoots, edits or posts property media in Ontario. The rules are set out in the *Trust in Real Estate Services Act, 2002* and its regulations, and RECO explains them in a series of advertising bulletins. Here is what those bulletins say that touches photos and video directly.

## Advertising is broader than you might think

RECO defines advertising as any public notice or representation made by or for a brokerage or agent to promote the brokerage, an agent, or their services. That covers print, broadcast, websites and social media. A listing gallery, a walkthrough video posted to Instagram and a reel of a sold home all fall inside it.

Two baseline requirements follow every one of those pieces. The brokerage has to be clearly and prominently identified, including in ads run by agents on a team. And where an agent is named, it has to be the name registered with RECO, with a permitted title such as salesperson or broker. Nicknames and short forms are not allowed.

For media, that usually means the brokerage name belongs on the branded video end card, the social caption or the account profile, not just on the MLS listing the post links to.

## A photo can identify a property, and that needs consent

RECO's advertising bulletin says you cannot include anything, image or text, that could reasonably identify a specific property without the owner's consent. The same goes for anything that could reasonably identify a party to a transaction.

For a new listing this is normally handled in the listing paperwork. Where it gets tricky is everywhere else media ends up:

- **Sold posts.** RECO's bulletin on sold property says a photo counts as identifying the property. Before closing, a "sold" ad from either brokerage needs the seller's consent. After closing, it needs the buyer's consent. If the ad shows the price or other terms, both parties have to consent.
- **Happy-buyer photos.** Showing buyers and their names needs their written consent.
- **Someone else's media.** Using another person's images, logos or word-for-word property descriptions needs the owner's written consent. Sharing showing footage of another agent's listing needs the seller's or tenant's permission, obtained through their agent, and the post has to credit the listing brokerage.

RECO also says consent should be in writing and, for sold ads, state clear effective and expiry dates.

## Media has an end date

This is the rule that catches people. RECO's online advertising bulletin says photos and video, including virtual showings, cannot stay online past the end date in the written consent. Brokerages and agents are expected to review their content regularly and take down what they no longer have permission to use. If a buyer or seller later asks for something to be removed, RECO says removing it promptly shows integrity and professionalism.

In practice, that means keeping track of where every gallery and video has been posted: the brokerage site, the agent's personal and business accounts, YouTube, and any third-party listing pages you control. RECO is clear that a third-party-managed website does not exempt a brokerage or agent from keeping information current and accurate.

## Accurate means accurate, in pictures too

RECO's bulletins require advertising to be factually correct, accurate and verifiable, and online content must not misrepresent facts. RECO separates three kinds of problem statements: false (factually wrong), misleading (leaves a wrong impression, even if it does not mislead everyone) and deceptive (purposely misleading).

The bulletins do not set separate photo-editing rules. But the same standard is a useful test for the edits a listing gallery goes through. Correcting exposure, straightening verticals and replacing a grey sky are generally about showing a room as it looks on a good day. Removing a hydro tower from the backyard view, hiding a cracked foundation wall, or furnishing an empty room without saying so starts to leave buyers with the wrong idea about what they are walking into. If an edit changes what a buyer would reasonably expect to find on a showing, it deserves a second look, and virtually staged images are best labelled as such.

## A short checklist for your next listing

- Confirm the seller's written consent covers photos, video, drone and virtual tour, and note its end date.
- Put the brokerage name, as registered, on every branded video and post.
- Get written consent before posting buyers, sellers or a sold price.
- Keep a list of everywhere the media lives so it can come down on time.
- Ask your photographer what was changed in editing, and label virtual staging.
