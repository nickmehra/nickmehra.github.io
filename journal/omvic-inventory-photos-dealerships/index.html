Inventory photos for Ontario dealerships: what OMVIC's advertising guideline expects from your images

Vehicle photos and videos are advertising under Ontario's dealer rules. Here is what OMVIC's Advertising Guideline says about stock photos, the actual unit, sold vehicles and social posts, and how to build an inventory shoot around it.

On a dealership website, the photo gallery is usually the first thing a shopper looks at and the last thing anyone on the lot thinks about. Units arrive, get detailed, get a quick walkaround on a phone, and go live. In a busy month, some never get their own photos at all.

In Ontario, those images are advertising. The Ontario Motor Vehicle Industry Council (OMVIC) regulates dealers and salespeople under the Motor Vehicle Dealers Act, 2002, and its Advertising Guideline sets out how the council reads the advertising rules. The guideline itself says it does not change the law, and that the legislation wins if the two ever conflict. But it is the clearest plain-language statement of what OMVIC expects, and several parts of it are about pictures.

Show the actual vehicle when you can

OMVIC's guideline says an ad should ideally show the actual unit being offered, including its colour and model year. That is the standard to aim for, and it lines up with what shoppers want anyway: they are deciding whether to drive across town to see a specific vehicle.

The council has held this position for a long time. A 2011 OMVIC dealer bulletin made the same point, acknowledged that photographing every advertised vehicle is not always practical, and said that for used vehicles the ad should show a photo of the specific vehicle for sale.

Stock photos are allowed, with conditions

Where the real unit is not photographed, the current guideline allows a stock or other photo only if it accurately reflects the make, model, trim level and condition, and the ad clearly states that the pictured vehicle is not the one for sale.

New vehicles get a specific warning. If the price comes from a manufacturer photo, the trim and colour shown have to be available at the advertised price. If the image shows a higher trim or a different colour, the ad has to say so and give the all-in price for the model pictured. OMVIC gives the example of a base-model price beside a photo of an upgraded trim and treats it as misleading.

That is easy to get wrong without meaning to. A manufacturer's hero image of a top trim, dropped into a template for an entry-level price, is exactly the case the guideline describes.

Photos have to keep up with the lot

The guideline says that if an ad shows a price, the vehicle has to be available at that price when the ad runs. Limited supply has to be stated, vehicles not yet in stock have to be disclosed (for example as available by factory order), and once a vehicle sells or is no longer available at that price, the ad has to be promptly removed or updated.

OMVIC's May 2026 notice on all-in pricing adds a related point about images: photos should reflect the vehicle's current condition, and misleading or outdated images can confuse buyers and lead to complaints. A gallery shot before a scrape, a missing wheel or a swapped set of tires is no longer an accurate picture of the car.

The price and the dealer travel with the image

Photos rarely run alone. Wherever they appear, the same baseline rules apply to the ad around them:

  • All-in price. If a price is shown, it must include every fee and charge the dealer intends to collect, such as freight, pre-delivery inspection, administration and safety. OMVIC says only HST and licensing may be left out, and the ad must clearly say so.
  • Dealer identification. Every ad must include the dealer's registered name and contact information that the Registrar has made public.
  • Past use. The guideline says ads must disclose if a vehicle was previously a daily rental (unless later owned by an individual), a police or emergency vehicle, or a taxi or limousine.

Social posts and marketplace listings count too

Salespeople often post inventory on their own accounts, Facebook Marketplace, Kijiji or Autotrader. OMVIC allows that, but the guideline says the salesperson needs the dealer's approval first, must include the required dealer information, and cannot make the post look like a private sale or send people anywhere other than the dealer's authorized premises. A short walkaround video on a personal account is still a dealer ad.

Building an inventory shoot around the rules

None of this calls for elaborate production. It calls for a routine:

  • Photograph each unit, not each model. A consistent set for every vehicle on arrival removes most of the stock-photo questions before they come up.
  • Capture what identifies it. Exterior colour, trim badging, wheels and the odometer help show the gallery is of the actual unit.
  • Record condition honestly. Close-ups of wear, chips or curb rash build trust and keep the gallery accurate.
  • Reshoot when something changes. New tires, repairs or damage on the lot should mean new photos.
  • Label anything that isn't the unit. If a stock or manufacturer image is used, say so in the ad, and check that its trim and colour match the price.
  • Take down sold units quickly, including on salespeople's social accounts.
A buyer who drives in because of a photo should find that exact vehicle waiting.
