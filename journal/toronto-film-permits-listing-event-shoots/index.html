Do you need a City permit to shoot in Toronto? What the Film Office rules mean for listing and event media

Toronto's Film Office treats real estate ads, special events and commercial stills differently. Here is when a City permit applies, when it does not, and what to plan for if your shoot spills onto streets, sidewalks or parks.

Most listing and event shoots happen indoors or on private land, and nobody thinks about the City until a tripod ends up on a sidewalk, a crew van needs a curb, or a drone needs somewhere to take off. In Toronto, that is where the Film Office comes in.

The City publishes its rules in two main places: the Guidelines for Filming in Toronto and the Plan Your Shoot pages. They are written mainly for film and television productions, but they also cover commercial stills, and they say plainly which kinds of shoots are not "commercial filming" at all. If you are a realtor, a seller or an event organizer working in the city, a few parts are worth knowing.

Real estate ads and special events are not "commercial filming"

The City's guidelines list several activities that do not count as commercial filming. Real estate advertisements are on that list, and so are special events, live event broadcasts, premieres, demonstrations and internal corporate training.

That cuts both ways. It means a listing shoot or event coverage is not something the Film Office issues a film permit for. But the guidelines also note that these activities may still need permits or approvals from other City divisions. "Not a film permit" is not the same as "no rules apply".

For most listing work the question never comes up: the shoot happens inside the home and on its lot. Events are similar when the venue is private: coverage happens inside the space the organizer has booked.

Private property: the default with no City permit

The Plan Your Shoot page says a film permit is not needed when everything, including parking and vehicles arriving and leaving, stays on private property and happens between 7 a.m. and 11 p.m. The property owner's permission is still required.

For a listing, that is the normal case. Photographer and gear arrive, park in the driveway, shoot the interior and exterior, and leave. Night work after 11 p.m., or anything involving special effects, falls outside that simple case.

When a shoot moves onto City property

Commercial stills are treated differently from listing ads. According to the guidelines, the Film Office issues permits for commercial stills photography on City streets and sidewalks, while commercial stills in City parks go through Parks, Forestry and Recreation.

The City uses a few permit types:

  • Grid permit. For roving, short exterior work on City right-of-way, with no more than one hour at any one spot. It does not cover parks or municipal squares and comes with no parking privileges.
  • Location permit. Needed when filming runs longer than an hour on City property, when streets are used to support filming on private property, or when drones, special effects or set dressing are involved.
  • Parks permit. For filming in City parks, coordinated with Parks, Forestry and Recreation.

The City's fee table lists a $100 production registration fee for commercial work, including stills, and a $150 location permit fee for on-street stills, among other charges. Fees can change, so check the current table before you budget.

Lead time, insurance and neighbours

Three practical points come up again and again in the City's material:

  • Apply early. The guidelines ask for at least two full business days, not counting the day you submit, and at least three for parks. Applications arriving after 4:30 p.m. are dated the next business day, so a Friday-afternoon request for a Monday shoot will not work.
  • Insurance. Before a location permit is issued, the City requires a certificate showing general liability coverage of at least $2,000,000 per occurrence, or more if the City reasonably asks, with the City of Toronto named as an additional insured.
  • Notify the street. For permitted location work, affected residents and businesses must get written notice at least 48 hours before the crew arrives. The Film Office reviews that letter first.

Drones

Drone flights are regulated federally by Transport Canada, and the City says so. Its Plan Your Shoot page adds that if you plan to take off or land on City property, including roads and parks, you should talk to your Film Permit Coordinator about the federal approvals involved. Adding drone work to an existing permit counts as a change and needs the same advance notice as other revisions. We covered the federal side in an earlier post on drone rules for listings.

A short planning checklist

  • Keep it on the lot when you can. Parking in the driveway and shooting from the property avoids most City questions.
  • Ask where the drone will launch. A backyard is private; a boulevard or park is not.
  • Flag street-side setups early. If a twilight exterior needs a tripod on the sidewalk for longer than a quick pass, raise it during planning, not on the day.
  • For events, check who holds the permit. Organizers using parks or squares should confirm what their own event permit covers before adding media on top.
  • Outside Toronto, check the local rules. These are City of Toronto rules; other municipalities, including those in Durham, handle filming on their property in their own way.

The point is not paperwork for its own sake. A shoot that is planned around these lines simply goes more smoothly on the day. If you have a listing or event coming up in Toronto or Durham, book a shoot.

This article is general information, not legal advice. City of Toronto requirements, fees and processes change; confirm the current rules with the Toronto Film Office or the relevant City division before your shoot.
