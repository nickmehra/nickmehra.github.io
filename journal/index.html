Notes from the restart

Short entries on leaving one career and building the next.

No. 16  ·  November 28, 2026

Making room for shadow

When I first picked up a camera seriously, I chased brightness. More light, fewer dark corners, everything visible. A good picture, I assumed, was one where nothing was hidden.

It took me a while to see that shadow is what gives a photograph its shape. Light on its own is flat. It is the darker side of a face, the falloff across a wall, the corner of a room the window does not reach, that tells you something has depth. Take the shadow away and the picture loses its form.

I think I had the same instinct about myself for a long time. In the work I did for almost two decades, you kept things squared away. You stayed composed, you kept the hard parts out of view, and you got on with it. That served a purpose, and I am not sorry for it. But it meant there were parts of me I never really looked at.

The darker parts of a picture are not the flaw. They are where the depth comes from.

The real change came when I finally turned and looked inward, without the usual hurry to tidy things up. It was a quiet reckoning. I looked at how I saw myself, the parts I was proud of and the parts I had avoided, and at what my life was meant to be about. I did not rush it, and it did not rush for me. But something shifted at the root. I came out of it seeing myself, my purpose and my life differently, and I can say plainly that it is what turned my life around.

What surprised me was that nothing had to be erased. The parts I had kept in the dark did not need to disappear. They needed to be seen honestly and given their place. A person, like a frame, holds together better when the light and the shadow are both allowed to be there.

That is how I try to shoot now. I do not fight every dark corner. I ask what it is doing for the picture. Sometimes it needs a little light. Often it just needs to be left alone, so the rest of the frame has something to rest against.

Being the odd one out taught me some of this early. I never quite fit the bright, simple version of what was expected. For years that felt like a flaw to hide. Now it feels more like contrast. It is part of the shape.

No. 15  ·  November 27, 2026

What the frame leaves out

Every photograph is a decision about what not to show. The frame has four edges, and everything outside them is left behind on purpose. When I started, I thought the skill was in what you pointed the camera at. More and more, I think it is in what you are willing to leave out.

That did not come naturally to me. For a long time my work rewarded taking everything in. You scan the whole scene, you account for every corner, you do not let anything slip past. Missing something could matter. So I learned to hold it all at once.

A camera asks for the opposite. Try to include everything and the picture says nothing. The eye does not know where to rest. The strongest frames are usually the simplest ones: one subject, clean edges, room to breathe.

Clarity is not about seeing more. It is about choosing what deserves to stay.

I am finding the same thing is true outside the frame. Building something on my own means a constant stream of possibilities, ideas, directions, things I could be doing. The temptation is to chase all of them at once. But the work gets sharper when I narrow it. Fewer things, done properly. A clear subject at the centre, and the discipline to keep the rest out of the shot.

Being the odd one out has given me some practice with this. I never fit everything that was expected of me, and for years I tried. Eventually I had to accept that a life, like a picture, holds together better when it is built around what actually matters to you instead of around everything you think should be in it.

None of this is about shutting things out of fear. It is closer to editing. You look honestly at what is in front of you, you keep what is true, and you let the rest go without making a drama of it.

I still take too many frames on most shoots. The cutting happens later, quietly, one image at a time. It is slow work. It is also where the picture really gets made.

No. 14  ·  November 26, 2026

Room to be wrong

In my old work, getting it wrong was not something you practised. You trained so that when it counted, you got it right. Mistakes were things to prevent, document and learn from afterward, quietly, and preferably not repeat.

Creative work asks for something I was not used to giving myself: room to be wrong on purpose.

A lot of what I shoot does not work. A frame that looked right in the viewfinder falls flat on the screen. An angle I was sure of turns out to say nothing. An edit I spent too long on gets thrown away. In the beginning, that felt like failing. It stung more than it should have, because I had spent years in a world where a miss was a problem.

You do not find the good frame by avoiding the bad ones. You find it by walking through them.

Slowly, I am learning that the misses are not a detour. They are the route. Every frame that does not work tells me something about the one that will. The only way to learn what light does on a surface, or how a space wants to be seen, is to try it, look honestly at the result, and try again.

The discipline from the uniform helps here, just not in the way I expected. It does not stop me making mistakes. It keeps me reviewing them properly instead of brushing them aside. I still look at what went wrong with the same care I used to bring to a report. The difference is that now I do it with curiosity instead of dread.

Being the odd one out prepared me for some of this. I have spent most of my life a little out of step, trying things my own way and getting some of them wrong in front of people. It never felt comfortable. But it taught me that being wrong for a while is survivable, and often it is how you end up somewhere true.

I still want to get it right. That part of me is not going anywhere. I just do not need to get it right the first time anymore.

No. 13  ·  November 25, 2026

The quiet before the shot

There is a moment on every shoot that nobody sees. The gear is set, the plan is clear, and for a few seconds before I press anything, I just stand there and look. No rushing. No checklist running in my head. Just the place, the light, and me paying attention to both.

I did not always know how to have that moment.

For years my mind was always a step ahead of where I was standing. Anticipating, scanning, preparing for what might happen next. That habit kept things safe, and I do not regret it. But it meant I was rarely all the way present anywhere. Even in quiet places, I was somewhere else.

Some things only become clear when you finally stop moving long enough to see them.

What changed that was not a technique. It was a deeper shift, further in. There was a time when I turned my attention inward and stayed there longer than was comfortable. I looked at how I saw myself, at what I had built my life around, and at what it was actually meant to be about. It was a quiet reckoning, mostly done in stillness, and it did not hurry for me. But it changed how I see myself and my purpose. I can say plainly that it turned my life around. Everything I am doing now grew out of that stillness.

The pause before the shot is a small echo of it. A few seconds where I stop leaning toward the next thing and let what is in front of me be enough. More often than not, that is when I notice what the picture is really about: a line of light on a wall, the way a room opens toward a window, a detail I would have walked straight past.

Creativity is still the catalyst. The discipline still gets me there prepared. But the best work seems to come out of that quiet in between, where I am not chasing anything, just looking.

I used to think stillness was wasted time. Now it is the part of the day I protect most.

No. 12  ·  November 24, 2026

What is in a name

People sometimes ask why I called the company BlackSheep. They usually expect a marketing answer. The honest answer is simpler: the name is true.

I have been the odd one out for as long as I can remember. At work, with friends, in my own family. Not loudly, and not on purpose. I just never quite matched the shape of the room. I was the one more interested in why things are the way they are than in the small talk around them, the one drawn to depth when everyone else was ready to move on.

For a long time I treated that as something to hide. In a uniform, fitting in is part of the job. You follow the procedure, you hold the line, you become part of something larger than yourself. There is real value in that, and I learned a great deal from it. But there was always a part of me standing slightly apart, watching, asking questions I kept to myself.

The thing I spent years trying to fix turned out to be the thing worth building on.

When I made the difficult decision not to go back, I knew whatever came next had to be honest. I did not want another role to step into. I wanted something that fit who I actually am. When the name came to me, it resonated somewhere deep. It was not clever. It was just accurate.

A black sheep notices things the flock walks past. It stands a little to the side, and from there it sees the whole field differently. That is close to how I try to work now. Look from a slightly different angle. Pay attention to what others overlook. Take the extra moment that the routine would skip.

The discipline still came from the uniform, and I carry it into every job. But the eye, the urge to see things my own way, was always mine. The name just gave it permission to lead.

I do not think I will ever fully fit the room. I have stopped wanting to.

No. 11  ·  November 23, 2026

Who you are without the uniform

A uniform does a lot of quiet work. It tells people who you are before you say a word. It tells you, too. For almost two decades I put one on and stepped into a role that already had its shape: what to say, how to stand, what was expected.

I did not realize how much of myself I had handed over to that shape until I stopped wearing it.

Without it, the first question is not what you do next. It is who you are when nothing outside you is answering for you. No title on your chest. No role to step into. Just you, and whatever is actually underneath.

A uniform can hold you up. It can also keep you from finding out what you would stand on without it.

I had started asking that question before the uniform came off. There was a stretch of my life where I turned inward and looked, honestly and without hurry, at how I saw myself. Not the role, not the reputation, but the person under them, and what my life was meant to be about. It was quiet work, mostly done in stillness, and it was harder than any shift. But it changed the ground I stand on. I see myself differently because of it, and I see my purpose differently. Looking back, that inner shift is what turned my life around. Everything since, including this, has grown out of it.

So when the uniform did come off, I was not starting from nothing. The discipline stayed. I still prepare properly, still show up early, still finish the job. What changed is where it comes from. It used to come from the role. Now it comes from something I chose.

Being the odd one out helps here, in a strange way. I never fit the shape of the room completely, even in uniform. There was always a part of me more interested in the bigger questions than the routine. I used to keep that part out of sight. Now it gets to lead.

The camera, oddly, makes this clearer. Behind it, nobody is looking at me. They are looking at what I noticed, and how I chose to frame it. That is a more honest way to be known than any title I wore.

I still have days where I miss the certainty of being told who to be. But I would not trade it back. I would rather be known for what I see than for what I wore.

Slow is not stuck

I used to think patience meant waiting things out. Standing a post, holding a position, getting through the hours until the shift was over. In that world, patience was mostly endurance. You did not have to like the waiting. You just had to stay sharp while it happened.

Building something new has asked for a different kind.

When you start from nothing, almost everything takes longer than you want it to. Skills take time to settle into your hands. Work takes time to find its people. A name takes time to mean something to anyone but you. For a while I measured each day by what had visibly moved, and on the days when nothing seemed to, I felt it.

Slow is not the same as stuck. Most growth happens where you cannot see it yet.

The camera has been a good teacher here. You cannot rush the light. You cannot hurry a scene into being ready. You can prepare, set up, and pay attention, and then the moment arrives in its own time. The photographers who get the best frames are rarely the fastest ones. They are the ones still standing there, paying attention, after everyone else has packed up.

That has changed how I think about my own progress. Patience is not passive. It is a way of staying present while the result is still out of reach. Doing the next useful thing, then the next, without demanding that each one pay off on the spot.

Being the odd one out helped, strangely. I have spent most of my life on a slightly different timeline from the people around me, wanting to talk about the bigger questions when everyone else had moved on. I learned early that my pace did not have to match the room. That turns out to be useful when you are building something on your own.

The discipline still comes from the uniform: show up, do the work, do it properly. What is new is the willingness to let things take the time they take, without reading the quiet stretches as failure.

Some days I still catch myself counting. But more often now, I notice the small signs that something is taking root. And I keep going.

No. 9  ·  November 21, 2026

Trusting the path

For most of my working life, the path was laid out in front of me. There were ranks, procedures, a next posting, a way things were done. You could see a few steps ahead, and if you did the work, you knew roughly where it led.

Building something of your own does not come with that. There is no map, only the next step and whatever you learn from taking it. Some mornings that feels like freedom. Other mornings it feels like standing at the edge of a field in fog.

I used to think certainty was the thing that made people steady. Know the plan, follow the plan, and the ground stays under your feet. I believed that for a long time, and in that work it served me well.

Steadiness does not come from knowing where the road goes. It comes from knowing why you are on it.

What changed that belief was not a career move. It happened further in, before any of this started. There was a stretch where I stopped looking outward for direction and started looking honestly at myself, at what I valued, at what my life was actually meant to be about. It was quiet, and it was not quick. But somewhere in that stillness I stopped needing to see the whole road. I found something more solid than a plan to stand on. Looking back, that shift is what turned my life around. Everything since has grown out of it.

So when the fog rolls in now, I do not panic the way I once might have. I do the part that is mine to do. I prepare properly, I show up, I do the work as well as I know how. The discipline still comes from the uniform. Then I let the rest unfold, and I pay attention to what it shows me.

It is a strange kind of trust. Not blind, and not passive. More like walking with your eyes open, knowing you will not see far, and going anyway because you know what you are walking toward.

I do not know exactly where this leads. For the first time, I am at peace with that.

Doing it properly

There is a phrase that stayed with me from the uniform years: do it properly. Not quickly, not impressively. Properly. Fill in the details. Check the thing you were fairly sure was fine. Finish the job the way it should be finished, even when nobody is going to look at the last part.

I did not expect that phrase to follow me into creative work. I assumed creativity would be the loose, open side of my life, and discipline would stay behind with the old job. It turns out they need each other more than I realized.

Most of what makes an image work is invisible. A line that is straight instead of almost straight. A colour that matches from one frame to the next. A distraction removed from the corner that no one would have named, but everyone would have felt. None of it is exciting. All of it matters.

Care is mostly made of small things nobody asks for.

Being the odd one out taught me something here too. When you are used to not quite fitting the room, you stop working for the room's approval. You start working toward a standard you hold privately. For years that felt isolating. Now it feels like a compass. I know when something is done properly, whether or not anyone else would notice the difference.

That does not mean perfection. Perfection is a trap, and it keeps you from ever finishing. Properly is different. It means honest effort, attention all the way through, and the humility to go back and fix what you missed.

I find a quiet satisfaction in that kind of work. There is a depth to it, the same depth I have always looked for in bigger questions. Doing something carefully is a way of saying it matters. And if it matters, it deserves to be done properly.

The bigger picture

When a drone rises above a property, the first thing that happens is that the details shrink. The front door, the garden, the car in the driveway all become small parts of something larger. You see the shape of the land, the street, the way one place sits beside another.

I never tire of that moment. It is a reminder that what looks huge from the ground is often just one piece of a much wider picture.

For a long time my life was lived at ground level. Close to the problem, close to the next call, close to whatever needed handling in the moment. That focus was necessary, and I was good at it. But it kept my eyes down. I rarely stepped back far enough to ask what all of it was adding up to.

Some answers only appear when you rise far enough to see the whole shape.

The change came quietly. At some point I started stepping back inside myself the way the camera steps back from the ground. Looking inward, slowly and honestly, at who I was and what my life was for. The bigger questions I had always carried, the ones about existence and meaning that made me the odd one out, stopped being a distraction and became the centre of things. That inner shift changed how I see myself and what I believe I am here to do. I am convinced it is what turned my life around.

I did not lose the ground-level eye. I still notice the small things, and the work depends on that. But now I hold them inside a larger frame. A detail matters more when you know what it belongs to.

That is what I try to bring to every piece of work now. Get close enough to care about the details. Then step back far enough to remember why they matter.

No. 6  ·  November 18, 2026

Discipline and the blank page

People sometimes assume discipline and creativity pull in opposite directions. One is rules, the other is freedom. One is the uniform, the other is the camera. I used to half believe that myself.

The longer I do this work, the less true it feels.

Creativity, for me, is the catalyst. It is the spark that makes me want to make something in the first place, the part that sees a possibility before it exists. But a spark on its own does not get very far. Left alone, it flickers, and then it is gone. What keeps it alive is structure. Showing up when you do not feel inspired. Charging batteries the night before. Reviewing the plan. Doing the dull parts so the interesting parts have room to happen.

Discipline is not the opposite of creativity. It is what gives creativity somewhere to land.

Almost two decades in security and law enforcement gave me a lot of structure. Routines, procedures, the habit of being prepared. When I left, I wondered whether I would need to shake all of that off to become creative. It turned out the opposite was true. The structure was not a cage. It was a foundation I could finally build something of my own on.

The blank page is still intimidating. It always will be a little. But I meet it differently now. I do not wait for inspiration to arrive fully formed. I start, because starting is a discipline, and more often than not the ideas show up once I am already moving.

There is a balance in it that I am still learning. Too much structure and the work goes stiff and predictable. Too little and it never gets finished. Somewhere between the two is a kind of focused freedom, where the habits carry the weight and the mind is free to wander and notice.

The uniform taught me how to show up. The camera is teaching me what to do once I am there.

No. 5  ·  November 17, 2026

Waiting for the light

The first thing a camera teaches you is that you are not really photographing things. You are photographing light. The house, the car, the face in front of you are only what the light happens to be landing on.

I knew that in theory before I started. I did not feel it until I had to wait for it.

For most of my working life, waiting meant something else. It meant standing by, staying alert, ready to move the second something changed. It was waiting with your shoulders up. Useful, and necessary in that work, but it never let you rest inside the moment. You were always leaning toward the next one.

Waiting for light is different. You cannot rush it and you cannot argue with it. A cloud moves when it moves. The sun drops when it drops. All you can do is be ready, be still, and pay attention while it happens.

Some things only show up when you stop trying to force them.

I think I recognized that feeling because I had already met it somewhere else. Before any of this, something in me had to go quiet for a while. I had to stop pushing and start looking inward, honestly, at who I was and what my life was actually for. It was not dramatic. It was slow, and mostly silent, and it asked more of me than anything the uniform ever did. But it changed how I see myself, and it changed the direction of everything after it. I do not think I would have found my way here without it.

What I learned in that stillness is the same thing the light keeps teaching me. The right moment is not something you take. It is something you make room for. You prepare, you show up early, you do the work, and then you trust it enough to wait.

The discipline still matters. It gets me to the location on time, with the gear checked and a plan. But the plan only gets me to the edge of the shot. The rest belongs to patience, and to a kind of quiet I used to think I had no use for.

These days the light goes early. I find I do not mind. Shorter days just mean you notice it more.

Working alone

For most of my career I was never really alone at work. There was always a partner, a supervisor, a dispatcher, a team on the same shift. Even on a quiet post, someone knew where I was and someone had my back. That is how the work is built, and for good reason.

Now most days it is just me. Me and the gear, the plan for the day, and whatever I decide to do with the hours in front of me.

I expected that to feel like freedom, and some of it does. What I did not expect was how much of the old structure I had been leaning on without noticing. When no one checks your work, you have to become the one who checks it. When no one sets the standard, you either set one yourself or slowly drift without one.

Alone does not mean unaccountable. It means the accountability moved inside.

This is where the uniform still helps. The discipline did not come from being watched. It came from repetition, from doing things properly when it would have been easier not to, until that became the default. I still prepare as if someone is going to review it. I still arrive early. I still finish the job the same way whether anyone sees the last part or not.

Being the odd one out turned out to be good training for this, too. I have spent a lot of my life slightly apart from the room, comfortable in my own company and in my own thoughts. I used to wonder if that was a flaw. Here, it is closer to a skill. I can sit with a long edit, with a quiet morning, with a question that does not have an answer yet, without needing to fill the silence.

That does not mean I want to do everything alone. Working on your own makes you more aware of how much other people bring. Their perspective catches what yours misses. I am learning to ask for that on purpose, instead of having it handed to me by a shift roster.

But I am also learning that some of the best work happens in that solitude. No noise, no hurry, just the work and the attention it deserves. For someone who has always found peace in depth, that is not a lonely place. Most days, it feels like home.

Starting from zero

There is a particular quiet that comes with starting over. No shift schedule. No radio. No one telling you where to be next. For almost two decades my days had a structure someone else built. Now the structure is mine to build, and the first thing I noticed was how loud the silence was.

Starting from zero sounds like a loss. Some days it feels like one. Experience does not show up on a portfolio. The years I spent in security and public service law enforcement do not count as listings shot or clients served. In this work, I am new, and there is no way around that except through it.

But zero is not the same as empty. I did not arrive here with nothing. I arrived with discipline, with the habit of showing up early, with an eye that learned to take in a whole scene before reacting to any part of it. The uniform gave me those. I just have to point them somewhere new.

What changed most was not the work. It was something further in. Somewhere along the way I stopped measuring myself by whether I fit in the room, and started asking a harder question: what am I actually here to do? I did not find the answer all at once. It came slowly, in stillness, more like a quiet reckoning than a decision. But once I started listening to it, the rest of my life began to rearrange itself around it. That shift is the real reason I am here, writing this, behind a camera instead of going back.

So I am learning to be patient with zero. Each frame is a small proof. Each finished piece adds a line to a record that did not exist a few months ago. The odd one out is used to building on his own terms. This time, the terms are mine.

No. 2  ·  November 14, 2026

Learning to notice

For most of my working life, noticing things was the job. In enforcement you learn to read a scene quickly. What is out of place, what has changed, what does not add up. You look for the problem because the problem is what you are there for.

I did that for almost two decades. It becomes part of how you see, whether you are on shift or not.

Behind a camera, I am still noticing. The habit did not leave with the uniform. But the question I am asking has changed. I am no longer looking for what is wrong. I am looking for what is there.

The same eye, pointed at a different question.

That shift sounds small. It is not. Looking for the problem keeps you guarded. Looking for what is there asks you to slow down and let a place or a person show you something before you decide what it means. Light falling a certain way. A detail most people walk past. The moment just before someone relaxes.

I have always been drawn to depth, to the bigger questions about why we are here and what any of it is for. For a long time that felt like a separate part of me, the part I kept quiet at work. Now I think it was the same instinct all along. Paying close attention is a way of taking something seriously. It says this matters enough to look at properly.

Some days I catch myself scanning a room the old way, out of habit. I do not fight it anymore. The discipline to keep looking came from the uniform, and I am grateful for it. What I am learning now is patience with what I find, to stay with it a little longer instead of moving on to the next thing.

Creativity, for me, starts there. Not with an idea, but with attention. You cannot make something honest out of a world you have not really looked at.

I am still learning to notice. I suspect I always will be.

No. 1  ·  November 2026

The odd one out

For almost two decades I worked in security and public service law enforcement. Private security first, then transit enforcement operations, then municipal law enforcement, then enforcement in a provincial capacity.

Through all of it I was the odd one out. At work, with friends, in my own family. I was the one more interested in the bigger questions than the small talk. For years I treated that as something to manage.

I have always found peace in depth. It took me a long time to see that as a strength.

In 2026 I had to decide whether to go back to enforcement. I decided not to. It was not an easy call and I will not pretend it was.

I started a company instead and called it BlackSheep Digital Works, because the name is true. Creativity is the catalyst for everything I do now. The discipline came from the uniform. The eye was always mine.

This journal is where I will write about the work, and about what it is like to start again.
