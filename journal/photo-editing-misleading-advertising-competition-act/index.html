How far can photo editing go? The Competition Act's general impression test for listing and dealership images

Canada's Competition Act looks at the overall impression an ad creates, not just its literal words, and that includes the pictures. Here is what the Competition Bureau says, and how to edit vehicle and listing photos without crossing the line.

Every professional photo is edited. Exposure gets balanced, colours get corrected, verticals get straightened. That is part of making a picture look like what you saw standing there. The question for dealers, agents and sellers is where ordinary editing stops and a misleading ad begins.

We have covered the industry-specific rules before: RECO's advertising bulletins for real estate and OMVIC's guideline for dealers. Underneath both sits a federal law that applies to almost every business that advertises in Canada: the Competition Act. The Competition Bureau's guidance on it has a few points that bear directly on images.

The general impression counts, not just the words

The Bureau explains that when a court looks at whether a representation is false or misleading, it considers the general impression it creates as well as its literal meaning. An ad can be accurate word for word and still mislead.

One of the Bureau's own examples is directly about pictures: a message whose text is literally true, paired with a photo or illustration that creates a false impression, such as an image of a different product from the one being advertised. Its list of dos and don'ts also warns against using illustrations that differ from the product being sold.

The Act also covers representations "in any form whatsoever", which is broad wording. The practical reading is simple: what the photo shows is part of what the ad says.

Fine print rarely fixes a misleading picture

The Bureau advises businesses to avoid fine-print disclaimers, noting that they often do not change the general impression. If a disclaimer is used, the ad and the disclaimer taken together must not leave a misleading impression.

For images, that means a small "photo for illustration purposes" line under a picture that shows something materially different is a weak defence. The better fix is a photo that shows the real thing.

"Material" is the key word

The Bureau says the Act applies to representations that are false or misleading in a material respect, meaning something that could influence a person's decision to buy. It also notes that nobody actually has to be deceived for an ad to be misleading, and that a business can be responsible for representations made by its employees.

That framing helps when deciding how far to edit. Removing a stray leaf, brightening a dim room or correcting a colour cast does not change what is being sold. Hiding a crack, a stain, a dent or a scratch does.

The stakes

The Act has both civil and criminal tracks. On the civil side, the Bureau lists penalties for a first occurrence of up to $750,000 for individuals and up to $10,000,000 for corporations, with higher maximums for later occurrences, along with orders such as restitution. The criminal provisions apply where a materially false or misleading representation is made knowingly or recklessly.

Most image problems never come near those numbers. They show up first as a buyer who drives across town and finds something different, then as a complaint.

A practical editing standard

Here is the line we work to, and it is a useful one to ask of any photographer or editor:

  • Fine: exposure and white balance, straightening lines, lens correction, removing temporary clutter (a garbage bin, a stray hose, a reflection of the photographer), balancing window light.
  • Disclose: virtual staging, a replaced sky that changes the feel of the scene, or a stock or manufacturer image used in place of the actual unit.
  • Don't: remove permanent features such as power lines, a neighbouring building or a utility box; hide damage, wear or defects; change a vehicle's colour, trim or wheels; make a room look larger than it is with extreme lenses or stretching.

The test is the general impression. If a buyer standing in front of the home or the vehicle would feel the photo promised something different, the edit went too far.
